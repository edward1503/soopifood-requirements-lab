# SoopiFood: Simulated Interview and Observation Notes

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA — NOT REAL RESEARCH**

All eight interviews (I1–I8) and both dinner-service observations (O1–O2) below are fictional material created for this tutorial using `interview_guide_v2.md`. No interviews or field observations took place. All sites, incidents, times, staffing details and order references are invented. They illustrate possible experiences, not findings about actual restaurant managers or evidence of how common a need is.

The interview notes are simulated paraphrases, not transcripts or verbatim quotations. They describe current work in the fictional settings; references to existing tools do not prescribe a SoopiFood design. No personas have been generated.

## How to use these notes

- Interview entries follow Q1–Q10 of the revised guide. Cite a whole interview as `[I1]`, or identify an individual response as `I1/Q2` when checking traceability.
- Observation entries have event IDs, such as `O1-E3`. Cite the session as `[O1]` and use event IDs to locate the supporting detail.
- O1 shares the fictional setting of I1; O2 shares the setting of I4. They are paired examples, not independent corroboration from real research.
- Interview notes describe what a fictional manager reports. Observation logs describe externally visible actions in a fictional scene. Follow-up questions are explicitly separated from both.
- Preserve uncertainty. An unmentioned behaviour is not proof that it never occurs, and a single incident is not a general pattern.

## I1 — Simulated interview: Site A, small neighbourhood takeaway

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Evening manager at a takeaway receiving delivery-platform, telephone and walk-in orders. On the described dinner shift, the manager works with two kitchen staff.

- **Q1 — Role:** Covers the counter, checks incoming orders, packs bags and hands them to riders. Kitchen staff prepare food. When a colleague is absent, the manager spends more time packing and less time watching incoming work.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A platform order arrived while a walk-in customer was paying. The manager read it, passed a handwritten ticket to the kitchen, checked the packed items and handed the bag to a rider who supplied the matching order reference.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses an existing tablet, paper tickets and spoken updates. Reports that the shared internet connection sometimes drops; during one interruption, kept a paper record of an order already received and checked its status after the connection returned. Does not know whether any unseen orders were waiting.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Finds preparation and handover straightforward when orders are spaced out. During the dinner rush, repeated counter interruptions make it harder to remember which bags have been checked.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** Caught a missing side dish during the final bag check. The rider waited while it was added. Regards the check as useful even though it briefly slowed handover.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Usually matches a spoken reference to a ticket. Early riders sometimes ask the manager for updates while the manager is serving someone else. Does not normally communicate with riders before they arrive.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Handles telephone questions about missing items or changed pickup arrangements. For a recent missing-item call, checked the retained ticket and arranged a replacement after confirming with the kitchen.
- **Q8 — Success:** Judges the shift by correct bags leaving the counter and the kitchen finishing without a backlog. Staff comments matter more than public ratings in this assessment because ratings rarely explain a particular shift.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Wants to preserve the final bag check and quick verbal coordination. Would first address losing track of checked versus unchecked bags when interrupted; regards this as more consequential than making a quiet shift faster.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Suggests hearing from a kitchen colleague about which spoken updates are useful. Has no written record of how often interruptions lead to rechecking.

## I2 — Simulated interview: Site B, family-run restaurant with telephone delivery orders

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Dinner manager coordinating a small kitchen, a dining area and local delivery collections. The manager is an employee, not the owner.

- **Q1 — Role:** Assigns work during service, answers the telephone when available and resolves order queries. Other staff take table orders. A local courier service collects delivery orders arranged by telephone.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A regular customer telephoned an order for collection by a courier. The manager wrote the customer's request on a numbered duplicate pad, read it back, passed one copy to the kitchen and kept the other at the counter. The order left with the courier after a reference check.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses the paper pad, telephone and a wall clock. Reports that this routine is dependable at the current order volume. Unclear requests are clarified with the customer before preparation rather than interpreted by the kitchen.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Much of the evening follows a familiar rhythm. Extra attention is needed when someone takes a telephone order while also carrying food to tables; staff normally ask a colleague to finish one activity first.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** An unusually large family order was collected without corrections after the manager read back the items and collection arrangement. Describes it as a good example of a familiar routine working, rather than a technology problem.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Calls the courier service once an approximate preparation time is known. A late collection usually leads to a telephone conversation. The manager cannot control how the courier service allocates drivers.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Often speaks directly with customers before an order is prepared. In a recent query about portion sizes, passed the telephone to a kitchen colleague who knew the dish better.
- **Q8 — Success:** Looks for customers receiving what they expected and staff completing the evening without unresolved queries. Values direct customer feedback but notes that regular customers may be more willing to speak than new ones.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Would keep the read-back practice and duplicate ticket. Places clearer responsibility for answering the telephone above replacing paper simply because it is paper.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Suggests speaking with the colleague who covers the telephone on the manager's day off. Does not set menu prices or negotiate courier contracts.

## I3 — Simulated interview: Site C, busy restaurant with rotating service staff

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Shift manager at a restaurant with dine-in and several existing delivery-order channels. Experienced and recently hired staff rotate between counter duties.

- **Q1 — Role:** Assigns counter coverage, coordinates with the kitchen lead and handles exceptions. Usually leaves routine order entry and packing to a designated colleague, but takes over when that person is occupied.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A counter colleague received an order containing a preparation note. The manager clarified the note with the kitchen, who confirmed they could follow it. The packer checked the note against the ticket before a rider collected the order.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses existing order screens, printed tickets and a shift notebook. Reports that experienced staff use abbreviations that newer colleagues sometimes interpret differently. The manager asks for clarification rather than treating an unfamiliar abbreviation as certain.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Work is usually smooth when the same colleague stays on the counter. Shift changes require more attention because responsibility for outstanding queries can be unclear.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** A new colleague asked about an ambiguous preparation note before sending it to the kitchen. The clarification delayed the start of that order slightly but avoided preparing it with the wrong assumption.
- **Q6 — Riders:** A counter colleague handles ordinary handovers. The manager becomes involved when a reference does not match or a rider cannot identify which collection they are making. Reports no need for personal contact with every rider.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Some questions arrive through an ordering service rather than directly. In one case the manager could not immediately reach the customer, so held preparation of the unclear item while seeking clarification through that service.
- **Q8 — Success:** Wants orders to be understood consistently across the shift, including by newer staff. Pays attention to questions raised by colleagues and to corrections needed before dispatch, without keeping a formal count.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Wants to preserve staff willingness to ask questions. Would prioritise clearer handover of unresolved matters and consistent language over increasing the number of orders handled at once.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Recommends interviewing both a new counter colleague and the kitchen lead. Has not established which abbreviations cause the most confusion.

## I4 — Simulated interview: Site D, pizzeria with a dedicated pickup counter

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Dinner manager at a pizzeria with a designated order coordinator, a packer and kitchen staff. Most delivery orders arrive through an established ordering system.

- **Q1 — Role:** Oversees service and deals with exceptions. The order coordinator handles incoming orders; the packer checks bags and collection references. The manager does not personally touch every order.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** An order containing pizzas and a cold side was printed. The kitchen prepared the pizzas, and the packer waited for all items before closing the bag. A rider collected it after the reference was checked. The manager was not involved.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses printed tickets, the existing order screen and a kitchen board. Reports that order receipt is generally reliable. Describes oven capacity and preparation sequencing as the more common constraints on busy evenings.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Staff handle spaced orders easily. Several large orders close together require coordination because preparing everything immediately can leave some food waiting while other items finish.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** An early rider waited while a large order was still cooking. Repeated requests for an update interrupted the coordinator, but the manager considered the wait preferable to handing over an incomplete order.
- **Q6 — Riders:** The packer handles references and collection. The coordinator gives an estimate when asked, based on a kitchen update. The manager says an estimate can change when the oven queue changes; it is not a guaranteed completion time.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Most routine questions go to the coordinator. The manager recently handled a complaint about food temperature after a late pickup, but could not establish the complete sequence after the food left the restaurant.
- **Q8 — Success:** Values food leaving complete and at an acceptable condition for collection, alongside reasonable waiting times. Considers staff accounts and complaints, while recognising that delivery outcomes are only partly visible to the restaurant.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Would keep the dedicated pickup roles and final completeness check. Would first improve how preparation expectations are coordinated during peaks rather than replace a receipt process that usually works.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Suggests talking to the oven cook and observing a busy pickup period. Has no reliable record separating kitchen delays from time spent waiting for collection.

## I5 — Simulated interview: Site E, full-service restaurant with a narrow shared counter

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Evening manager overseeing table service while also supervising takeaway and delivery orders. Front-of-house staff share a small counter with collection activity.

- **Q1 — Role:** Manages the dining room, supports staff and intervenes in delivery-order problems. A server usually checks incoming orders, but that responsibility can move when tables need attention.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** An order was received during a table-payment query. A server passed it to the kitchen, then returned to the dining room. The manager later checked that it had been prepared after a rider arrived looking for it. It was collected after the manager confirmed the bag contents.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses an existing ordering device, printed kitchen tickets and verbal updates. Often reads information while standing at the counter between other tasks; does not sit down to supervise delivery orders continuously.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Quiet periods are manageable. When table service and collections peak together, the manager needs to establish who last dealt with each outstanding delivery order.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** A bag placed beside a dine-in receipt was nearly treated as ready for collection before its drinks were added. A colleague stopped the handover. The manager attributes the confusion to shared space and an interrupted check, not to a confirmed device fault.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Riders and takeaway customers queue at the same counter. The manager sometimes asks them to wait while a staff member checks a reference; limited space makes simultaneous collections harder to organise.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Handles unresolved questions after a server has checked the ticket. In one recent case, the manager explained a delay directly to a waiting takeaway customer while another colleague continued table service.
- **Q8 — Success:** Wants both dining-room guests and collection customers to receive attentive service without incomplete orders leaving. Staff feedback helps identify where one activity displaced another.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Would preserve the ability for colleagues to cover one another. Prioritises making responsibility for outstanding work clear when duties change, followed by reducing confusion at the shared counter.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Suggests observing the counter during table turnover. Does not know how much floor space could be reassigned, because layout decisions belong to the owner.

## I6 — Simulated interview: Site F, small kitchen centred on scheduled evening collections

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Service manager at a kitchen that receives many advance orders for agreed collection windows, with a smaller number of same-evening orders.

- **Q1 — Role:** Reviews the evening's expected work with the kitchen, coordinates packing and checks collection arrangements. Two colleagues can cover packing, so the manager is not always stationed at pickup.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A customer ordered in advance and arranged courier collection for an agreed window. Staff added the order to the preparation list, packed it near collection time and checked the collector's reference. The manager only confirmed a later change to the collection arrangement.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses an order list, printed labels and telephone messages. Reports that the current tools are adequate when the day's information stays unchanged. Messages received after the preparation list is printed require explicit checking with the kitchen.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Advance work makes much of the evening predictable. Late changes demand attention because staff may already be following the printed list. An uneventful shift is common and is considered a good result.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** A courier was delayed, but the kitchen learned about it before finishing the last items. Staff adjusted the sequence and avoided leaving the whole order packed for a long wait. The manager regards the timely conversation as helpful.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Contact varies. Some collectors arrive within the agreed window without prior conversation; others telephone about changes. The manager does not assume an arrival time remains accurate after a courier reports a delay.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Most contact concerns collection arrangements rather than complaints. A recent request to move a collection later was checked with the kitchen before the manager confirmed it.
- **Q8 — Success:** Looks for food being ready close to collection, accurate packing and manageable work for the kitchen. Customer feedback is occasional; staff experience of the preparation sequence is more immediately available.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Would keep advance planning and the practice of confirming changes with the kitchen. Gives priority to ensuring everyone works from the same current arrangement rather than encouraging more last-minute orders.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Suggests comparing an ordinary evening with one containing many late changes. Has no agreed maximum number of changes the kitchen can absorb.

## I7 — Simulated interview: Site G, larger restaurant with delegated delivery operations

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Duty manager at a larger restaurant. A delivery team lead coordinates the pickup area; the duty manager oversees the whole service and receives escalations.

- **Q1 — Role:** Allocates staff across service areas and resolves matters that exceed the delivery lead's authority. Is not usually responsible for routine acceptance, packing or collection checks.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A rider disputed whether an order had already been collected. The delivery lead checked the available ticket and asked the manager for help. The manager contacted the ordering service; collection was held until the service clarified what to do. The manager did not witness the original handover being discussed.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses shift notes, the delivery lead's updates and existing order records. Finds these sufficient for ordinary oversight, but reports that records do not always explain who made a particular exception decision.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Routine delivery activity can continue without the manager watching it. During several simultaneous escalations, the manager spends time reconstructing events and deciding which issue needs attention first.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** A delivery lead resolved a straightforward item query within their authority and reported it at handover. The manager appreciated that service continued without involving them unnecessarily.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Usually has no direct contact with riders. When an issue is escalated, asks the delivery lead what has already been checked rather than restarting every routine check personally.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Receives escalated complaints, sometimes well after an order leaves. In a recent complaint, could verify the packed items from a staff check but could not independently verify the journey or final delivery.
- **Q8 — Success:** Values consistent decisions across shifts and staff knowing when to escalate. Reviews unresolved cases at shift handover; does not equate the absence of complaints with proof that every delivery went well.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Wants to preserve delegation. Would prioritise a clearer account of exceptions and ownership of unresolved cases, rather than receiving every routine update personally.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Says a delivery lead would give a better account of routine pickup than the duty manager. The manager's interview should therefore not be treated as a complete description of that workflow.

## I8 — Simulated interview: Site H, restaurant with irregular delivery demand

**SIMULATED TUTORIAL DATA.** Evening manager at a restaurant where dining-room and telephone takeaway business are steady but delivery collections are sporadic. Staffing includes colleagues who work only selected shifts.

- **Q1 — Role:** Coordinates table service, accepts telephone takeaway orders when available and checks unusual collection arrangements. Delivery work is shared rather than assigned to a permanent specialist.
- **Q2 — Recent order:** A customer telephoned a takeaway order and later sent a courier instead of collecting it personally. The manager confirmed the order reference and collection arrangement before releasing the bag. Describes this as an occasional case, not the usual workflow.
- **Q3 — Tools and information:** Uses a telephone, order pad and existing till. Finds the familiar routine sufficient for most shifts. After time away, occasional staff ask colleagues about collection arrangements they have not encountered before.
- **Q4 — Across the shift:** Routine takeaway work is familiar. Unusual requests can require discussion even on a quiet evening; the manager does not view difficulty as purely a function of the number of orders.
- **Q5 — Memorable incident:** Two colleagues gave different answers about who could authorise a change after an order was prepared. The manager settled the case, then explained the decision at the next handover. Has not checked whether every occasional colleague heard that explanation.
- **Q6 — Riders:** Most takeaway customers collect themselves. Has limited experience of courier problems and would not offer a general account of rider behaviour based on the few encounters remembered.
- **Q7 — Customers:** Usually talks directly with customers about their telephone orders. When a prepared order was changed, explained the available options after checking with the kitchen rather than promising a change immediately.
- **Q8 — Success:** Wants dependable service regardless of which colleagues are on shift. Relies on staff questions and direct customer conversations; does not use a formal delivery-performance measure.
- **Q9 — Keep or change:** Would keep the familiar routine for ordinary orders. Gives priority to consistent decisions for uncommon situations and helping occasional staff find out what applies.
- **Q10 — Further context:** Recommends interviewing an occasional colleague and observing another shift before assuming the manager's account describes everyone's experience. Delivery volume has not been counted systematically.

**Limits of this simulated scene:** No customer journey, hidden kitchen activity or activity after collection is shown. **Questions for a later interview, not findings:** What does a folded ticket mean to each colleague? Was restarting the interrupted check deliberate? What information was available during the loading screen? How often, if ever, does an interruption lead to a missed item?

**Limits of this simulated scene:** The log shows separate pickup roles and visible handover checks. **Questions for a later interview, not findings:** How are preparation estimates formed and revised? What determines which items are prepared together? How are early arrivals distinguished from delayed food? Under what circumstances does the coordinator ask the manager to intervene?
